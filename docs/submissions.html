#3 | Freeform Preference Learning for Robotic Manipulation

Marcel Torne Villasevil (Stanford)*; Anubha Mahajan (Stanford); Abhijnya Bhat (Stanford); Chelsea Finn (Stanford)
Abstract: Reward design remains a central bottleneck for autonomous robot policy improvement, especially in long-horizon manipulation tasks where sparse success labels provide too little signal and binary preferences collapse many competing notions of quality into one ambiguous signal. We introduce Freeform Preference Learning (FPL), a method for learning robot policies from freeform human preferences. Rather than asking annotators which of two trajectories is better overall, FPL lets them define natural-language preference axes, such as speed, safety, quality of placement, or carefulness, and provide pairwise preferences along each axis. These annotations are used to learn a language-conditioned reward model that maps a trajectory and preference label to an axis-specific reward. We use this model to train a reward-conditioned policy that optimizes across the multiple human-specified dimensions. Across four real-world and two simulated long-horizon manipulation tasks, FPL improves over sparse-reward and binary-preference methods by 38 percentage points. Beyond improved performance, FPL learns dense progress signals without explicit subtask segmentation, shows compositionality of behavior not present in the data, and allows users to steer the policy towards different behaviors at test time without retraining.

#5 | Mechanistic Specialization Does Not Guarantee Performance: Evidence from Dual Attention Transformers

Jonathan Scott (University of California, Santa Cruz)*
Abstract: Dual Attention Transformers (DATs) extend decoder-only Transformers with a dedicated relational-attention stream, making them a natural architecture for abstract identity rules such as ABA and ABB. Surprisingly, we find that comparably sized GPT-2 models outperform DATs on these tasks. We investigate this gap with two complementary mechanistic analyses. First, causal mediation analysis shows that DATs exhibit stronger evidence of hypothesized symbolic mechanisms: symbol abstraction, symbol induction, and retrieval, than GPT-2. Second, a routing analysis shows why this specialization does not translate into better behavior: DATs make more wrong-copy errors, can attend to the correct source token while still predicting the wrong token, and show weak direct contribution from relational attention to the correct-versus-wrong output margin. Ablating positive-routing heads hurts performance, while amplifying those heads improves DAT more than matched controls. These results show that explicit relational inductive biases can shape internal organization without guaranteeing task success.

#6 | Behavior-Based User Segmentation via Listwise Learning-to-Rank Trees

Yang Liu (Meta)*
Abstract: We present Behavior-based User Segmentation (BUS), a novel tree-based data structure that hierarchically partitions users guided by product-specific engagement behaviors to generate interpretable user segments for recommendation. BUS reformulates user attribute-based recommendation as a listwise learning-to-rank problem, optimizing Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) to ensure aggregated active-user behaviors effectively represent marginal (cold-start) users. A greedy construction algorithm with a novel regress operator filters irrelevant attributes at each iteration, guaranteeing monotonic loss reduction. We further extend BUS with connection-aware segmentation via social graphs to improve fairness and diversity. Deployed at industrial scale serving billions of users daily, BUS achieves significant online improvements in music ranking (+0.126% daily active producers) and email notification click-through rates (+2.95%). To our knowledge, BUS is the first listwise learning-to-rank framework for tree-based recommendation that integrates diverse categorical attributes with semantic interpretability at industrial scale.

#8 | Many Minds from One Model: Bayesian-Inspired Transformers for Population Diversity

Diji Yang (University of California Santa Cruz)*; Yi Zhang (University of California Santa Cruz)
Abstract: Despite their scale and success, modern transformers are usually trained as single-minded systems: optimization produces a deterministic set of parameters, representing a single hypothesis about the data. We propose Population Bayesian Transformers (B-Transformers), which enable sampling diverse yet coherent transformer large language model instances, referred to as "minds", from a single pre-trained LLM. B-Transformers introduces a Bayesian-inspired posterior proxy by injecting stochasticity directly into normalization layers, avoiding the prohibitive cost of full Bayesian neural networks or multi-model ensembles. During the generation of each response, we sample a single realization and hold it fixed, ensuring temporal consistency and reasoning coherence. Experiments on zero-shot generation and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) show greater semantic diversity, additional valid reasoning paths, and consistently improved RLVR performance compared with fixed-parameter baselines.

#12 | Reduced-Rank Regression trees: a parsimonious model for nonlinear multi-output regression

Kuat Gazizov (UC Merced)*; Miguel Carreira-Perpiñán (UC Merced)
Abstract: Decision trees with linear leaves construct piecewise-linear mappings and have long been recognized as powerful tools for nonlinear regression. However, they produce large models with many parameters and can easily overfit. We propose the Reduced-Rank Regression tree (RRR tree), a model that explicitly enforces a low-rank structure on the linear mapping at each leaf. Besides reducing the number of parameters considerably, this has the advantage of introducing a form of regularization that improves the generalization ability of the model, and it makes it possible to learn deeper trees that adapt better to nonlinear data manifolds. It also capitalizes on the fact that the rank of the mapping can be globally high but locally low, which is a significant improvement over the traditional, linear RRR model. To train an RRR tree, we introduce an algorithm based on alternating optimization, which jointly learns the tree-structured partition of the space and the low-rank leaf parameters. This approach scales with respect to data dimensionality and sample size. In extensive experiments, we demonstrate that RRR trees achieve strong predictive performance while producing models that are orders of magnitude smaller and faster for inference than established baselines.

#13 | The Spec–Test Coverage Gap: Quantifying Test-Level Overspecification in Issue-Resolution Benchmarks

Avi Aggarwal (Purdue)*
Abstract: SWE-bench and similar benchmarks decide whether an agent fixed a bug by running a test patch that the maintainer wrote alongside the official fix. Because that test was written by someone who already knew how they intended to fix the bug, it often checks more than the issue asked for: a specific error string, a specific return value from an internal helper, or a code path the issue never mentions. An agent that fixes the reported bug in a reasonable way can still be scored as a failure. Prior work has studied issues that are too vague; we study the other side, tests that are too specific. We define the Spec–Test Coverage Gap (STCG), a per-task rating of how much of what a test checks could have been worked out from the issue text alone. STCG is a four-level rubric, running from behavior the issue states outright to assertions that lock in one particular implementation, applied by an LLM judge that reads the issue and the test patch but never the official code patch. On 12 hand-labeled SWE-bench tasks drawn from six repositories and stratified across the four levels, a Gemini-2.5-Pro judge reaches 0.71 quadratic weighted kappa and lands within one level of the expert label on 11 of 12 tasks. We treat this as a feasibility check, and set out what would be needed to use STCG to audit a benchmark.

#15 | An Expectation-Maximization Perspective on Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning

Tianbing Xu (Waymo)*
Abstract: Reinforcement learning has become a central tool for improving the reasoning abilities of large language models, yet widely used methods such as PPO and GRPO rely on likelihood-ratio correction, clipping, and other stabilization mechanisms. We ask whether reasoning models can instead be trained from a simpler optimization principle. We show that reinforcement learning over latent reasoning trajectories naturally admits an Expectation-Maximization~\citep{em} formulation. The E-step constructs a reward-weighted distribution over sampled rationales, while the M-step performs reward-guided policy optimization. This perspective naturally yields a simple off-policy policy gradient that requires neither trajectory-level importance weighting nor clipping, and it admits a monotonic-improvement guarantee under exact E- and M-steps. On GSM8K and MATH Hard, EM Policy Gradient achieves performance comparable to or better than GRPO across multiple Qwen2.5 model scales. The results suggest that latent-variable optimization offers a simple and theoretically grounded alternative foundation for reinforcement learning in reasoning language models.

#16 | RideWay: Benchmarking Efficient Task Completion for Tool-Using Language Agents

#21 | Learning Spatial Immune Architecture from Routine H&E Predicts Histologic Subtype and Survival in NSCLC

Prisha Bhanushali (None)*
Abstract: Predicting immunotherapy response in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is an open problem: only ~20% of patients respond, and the standard PD-L1 biomarker counts marker-positive cells while ignoring the spatial organization of the tumor immune microenvironment. We ask whether clinically meaningful spatial signal can be recovered from the H&E whole-slide images already collected for every patient—without transcriptomics or specialized staining. Our pipeline combines (1) deep-learning nucleus segmentation (StarDist) over ~500,000 cells across 395 regions from 82 NSCLC patients; (2) extraction of 19 per-cell morphological, intensity, and spatial features; (3) tumor/immune/stromal classification validated against expert annotations (up to 50.7% tumor co-localization); and (4) region-level spatial descriptors—tumor-to-immune distance, local entropy, cell density—plus a composite immune-architecture score. This direction is opposite to the "immune-hot is favorable" prior, suggesting proximity may capture exhausted rather than active T cells—a signal invisible to bulk transcriptomics. Interpretable spatial features from routine images thus yield subtype- and survival-relevant predictions: a low-cost, scalable direction for computational pathology. We note limitations (small survival cohort, rule-based classification) and next steps toward learned classifiers and larger validation.

Abstract: LLM-based agents increasingly require external memory to operate across different sessions and users, but existing agentic memory systems bundle many design choices, making it difficult to tell which components help and which hurt. We present a controlled, single-variable ablation of five retrieval topologies over a shared extraction and embedding pipeline, evaluated on the LongMemEval and BEAM benchmarks. Our preliminary findings include: (1) topology rankings change across benchmarks, with co-mention expansion winning on sparse conversation histories but typed-edge routing winning on dense ones, (2) a systematic failure mode where graph-expanded candidates are buried by the cross-encoder reranker due to missing path traversal context, addressable by a simple path-injection technique, and (3) edge-only memory storage performing more weakly on most question types but uniquely scoring on temporal, summarization, and contradiction categories. These observations suggest that the right topology depends on the workload, which might be hidden by single-benchmark evaluations.

#151 | LLM Chess: Benchmarking Reasoning and Agentic Reliability through Full-Game Play

Sai Kolasani (UC Berkeley); Maxim Saplin (Independent Researcher); Nicholas Crispino (UCSC)*; Kyle Montgomery (UCSC); Jared Quincy Davis (Stanford); Matei Zaharia (UC Berkeley); Chi Wang (AG2); Chenguang Wang (UCSC)
Abstract: We introduce LLM Chess, an evaluation framework that probes the generalization of reasoning in large language models (LLMs) by having them play complete games of chess against a weak random move player and a chess engine opponent of variable strength. We play 200 configurations of 159 models and rate them on a single Elo scale, scoring separately how much of each game a model is able to finish. Because every position is generated during play and difficulty is controllable, the benchmark can resist saturation, as shown by two years of continuous evaluation. The best performing model still only reaches 1548 Elo, about half way up the chess engine opponent’s calibrated ladder.

#152 | Cracking Reasoning in Diffusion Models: Evolution Strategies Sidestep the limitations of Reinforcement Learning in Diffusion Large Language Models

Roberto Dailey (Cognizant AI Lab)*
Abstract: Diffusion Large Language models represent a new frontier for language modeling research, allowing for bidirectional reasoning and faster inference than standard autoregressive models. Yet their post-training pipeline remains bottlenecked by something they cannot provide: unbiased token sequence log-probabilities. Numerous attempts have been made at working around this limitation within a reinforcement learning paradigm, yet methods either introduce biases that hurt performance, or introduce structures that cannot be extended to long-horizon workflows. This preliminary work shows that these limitations can be side-stepped by using Evolution Strategies as a replacement for existing RL methods, achieving high performance while being naturally extendable to multi-hop, long-horizon, and multi-agent learning environments.

#154 | A Powerful Probe of the Cosmos: Agentic Strong Gravitational Lens Modeling

Sean Xu (UC Berkeley)*; Xiaosheng Huang (Santa Clara University); Aniket Jain (UC Berkeley)
Abstract: Strong gravitational lensing is on the cusp of a new, data-rich era. New wide-field survey telescopes are expected to discover and confirm hundreds of thousands of new gravitational lenses, $\sim1000\times$ more than the number of confirmed lenses today. These gravitational lenses provide insight into the expansion history of the universe, and the nature of dark matter and dark energy. Unlocking these insights requires large-scale, accurate modeling of these systems. Each lens model must be fast and statistically robust, enabled by the advent of highly parallelized, differentiable lens modeling codes running on GPUs. Even with such codes, however, human inspection is required to ensure convergence and physicality of the model. We demonstrate the ability of frontier large language models (LLMs) to produce successful lens models with minimal human intervention. This will lead to an automated pipeline to model $O(10^5)$ strong lens to measure the properties of dark matter and energy.

#155 | Harness as Model: Training Executable Agent Infrastructure from Verifier-Grounded Traces

Aria Shi (Santa clara university)*; shanglin yang (Google); li yin (sylph.ai); Haebin Seong (sylph.ai); Haoran Zhang (sylph.ai)
Abstract: A language-model agent is a fixed base model wrapped in software: prompts, tools, execution policies, context management, retries, and the agent loop itself. That wrapper is where most engineering effort goes and where most failures live, yet it is treated as infrastructure rather than as something learnable. The obstacle is not that harness state is unlearnable but that improving it safely is hard: the object is executable code that must survive a real environment, the evidence for what to change is buried in traces rather than exposed as a loss, the reward comes from a verifier that must stay unreachable by the thing being optimized, and infrastructure failures mimic capability failures. We propose \emph{Harness as Model}, giving agent infrastructure a model-like lifecycle and report two implementations. The first treats declared prompt and code parameters as trainable state, updated transactionally against a benchmark verifier. Its outcome motivates the second: we argue the binding limit is the \emph{proposal channel}, since a structured call filling a declared slot cannot add a tool, restructure a loop, or read the trace that would say which to do. The second therefore makes the candidate a whole harness module and the proposer a coding agent with trajectory access, selects by a frontier over per-task results, and adds two safeguards: rejecting candidates that encode benchmark knowledge, and classifying each trial by whether the harness was exercised before its reward counts. We contribute the lifecycle, a safety policy keeping measurement outside the search space, the diagnosis relating proposal expressiveness to what can be learned, and evidence that a trajectory-reading proposer surfaces defects a parameter-leveloptimizer structurally cannot.

Submission guidelines

All submissions must be made before the deadline to Microsoft CMT.

Please use the Latex template for NeurIPS, available in this link: NeurIPS format

While abstracts can be based on work that has been published elsewhere, the abstract should be original text of a self-contained idea and not copy-paste from a previously accepted paper.

All submissions must be anonymized and must not include any information that could intentionally or unintentionally compromise the double-blind review process. Authors may share versions of their work on preprint platforms such as arXiv. The conference will be held in-person. At least one author for each accepted abstract is requested to physically attend the conference to present their work.

The organizers will desk reject submissions based on the following criteria: * Have formatting issues, e.g., not following the formatting guidelines or template. * Exceed the 2 page limit. This includes using hyperlinks for extended content. * Have non-anonymized submissions.

High quality abstracts satisfactorily address the following:

  • Motivation & Problem Framing
    • Is the problem clearly stated?
    • Is there a compelling reason this work matters (scientifically or practically)?
  • Novelty / Idea Contribution
    • Does the work introduce a new idea, perspective, or research question?
  • Clarity and Organization
    • Is the abstract understandable?
    • Is the abstract well-structured and relatively self-contained?
  • Results / Proofs / Findings
    • Does the abstract include evidence?
    • Evidence includes but is not limited to experiments, qualitative results, case studies, examples, analysis, etc. Preliminary results are acceptable (and encouraged)
  • Conclusions and Discussion
    • Does the work provide any conclusions, insights or lessons?
    • Does this work invite discussion or debate? Would this spark conversation at the event?
  • Broad Applicability
    • Why should the broader ML community care about this? What is the broader impact of this work?
    • Is this exciting to a larger audience?
